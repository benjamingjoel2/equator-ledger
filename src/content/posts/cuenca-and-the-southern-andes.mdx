Cuenca announces itself from a distance. Come into the old centre along almost any street and, sooner or later, the rooftops part and there they are: the great blue-tiled domes of the New Cathedral, catching the light above a city of white walls, red roofs, wrought-iron balconies and flower-filled courtyards. It is a city that makes you want to slow down, which is lucky, because that is exactly how it is best enjoyed.

Where Quito is big, busy and dramatic, Cuenca is compact and walkable, a place for long breakfasts, unhurried church visits, evening walks by the river and afternoons browsing for hats. Around it, the southern Andes offer wild, lake-studded moorland in Cajas National Park and Ecuador's best-known Inca site at Ingapirca. Here is how to make the most of it.

## The old centre

The historic centre of Cuenca, formally Santa Ana de los Ríos de Cuenca, was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1999, according to [Wikipedia's list of World Heritage Sites in Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Ecuador). It is a pleasure to walk. Streets run in a neat grid, the buildings are low enough to let the sky in, and the architecture mixes colonial churches and convents with grand later façades, painted wooden doors and the occasional glimpse into a courtyard full of plants.

The heart of it all is **Parque Calderón**, the main square, shaded by trees and framed on one side by the Old Cathedral and on the other by the New Cathedral. Sit on a bench for half an hour and you will see a good slice of city life: families, students, people heading to work, flower sellers nearby. It is the natural place to start and finish each day.

### The New Cathedral and its blue domes

The New Cathedral is Cuenca's signature building and the image on most postcards of the city. From the square, its brick front is imposing; from higher ground, its blue domes are what make the skyline unmistakable. Inside, the space is vast and calm, with light filtering through stained glass. If access to the roof or upper levels is open when you visit, the view over the domes and the city is worth the climb, but arrangements change, so ask locally.

The Old Cathedral across the square is smaller and older, and the contrast between the two says a lot about how the city has grown. Beyond them, Cuenca has many more churches, monasteries and small museums. As in Quito, pick a few and give each one time rather than trying to see everything.

## The rivers

Cuenca is a city of rivers, and the **Tomebamba** is the one you will get to know. It runs along the southern edge of the old centre below a high bank known as El Barranco, where old houses perch above the water. Steps lead down from the old town to riverside paths lined with trees, and a walk along the Tomebamba, especially in the late afternoon or early evening, is one of the simplest and best things to do in the city.

Cross one of the bridges and you are into the newer side of Cuenca, with parks, cafés and wider streets. It is a good place to stretch your legs after a morning of churches.

## Markets and food

For a sense of daily life, head to a market. **Mercado 10 de Agosto** in the centre is a good one to start with: stalls piled with fruit, vegetables, flowers and herbs, and a cooked-food section where you can eat alongside people on their lunch break. The food of the southern Andes is hearty and built on pork, potatoes, corn and cheese, much like the rest of the highlands. Look out for roast pork, which you will find in markets across the Andes, and for the corn-based snacks and tamales that turn up at breakfast and in the late afternoon. Soups are a fixture of lunch.

As anywhere, eat where it is busy and the food is cooked fresh. The FCDO's health advice is to drink [boiled, filtered or bottled water](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/health). Keep an eye on your bag and phone in crowded markets.

### Toquilla straw hats

Cuenca is famous for toquilla straw hats, the finely woven hats that much of the world calls "Panama" hats even though they are made in Ecuador. The weaving of toquilla straw has been listed by UNESCO as intangible heritage. In Cuenca you can visit workshops and shops where hats are finished, blocked and shaped, and watch how the fine weave comes together. Quality, and price, varies enormously with the fineness of the weave, so take your time, try several on, and buy the one that feels right rather than the one you are told is the best.

## Cajas National Park

West of the city, **Cajas National Park** is the southern Andes at their wildest: open, windswept páramo (high Andean grassland) scattered with lakes, low twisted woodland in sheltered hollows, and wide skies that change by the minute. Even a short visit feels like a different world from the warm courtyards of the old centre.

You can do Cajas as a half-day or full-day trip from Cuenca. Walking trails range from gentle lakeside strolls to longer routes over rough, boggy ground, and weather can turn quickly, with mist, cold wind and rain. A guide is a good idea, both to choose a walk that suits you and because paths are not always obvious when cloud comes down.

Cajas is higher than the city, so take the altitude seriously. The FCDO notes that [altitude sickness is a risk in parts of Ecuador](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/health), and that UV radiation is a risk too. Walk slowly, drink water, use sun cream and a hat, and bring waterproofs and warm layers even if the morning in Cuenca is sunny. If you are coming to Cuenca from the coast or the Amazon rather than from Quito, give yourself a day or two in the city before heading up.

## Ingapirca

North of Cuenca, **Ingapirca** is Ecuador's best-known Inca site, and it makes a rewarding day trip. The setting is green, rolling highland farmland, and the site's centrepiece is a finely built stone structure that shows the Inca skill with fitted stonework. The site also tells the story of the Cañari people who lived in this region before the Inca, and the mix of the two cultures is part of what makes it interesting.

Ingapirca is best seen with a guide, who can explain what you are looking at; without one, the stones can be hard to read. It can also be visited on the way between Cuenca and the central Andes, which turns a long road day into something more interesting.

## Getting there

Cuenca has its own airport, [Aeropuerto de Cuenca](https://aeropuertocuenca.ec/), within the city itself. Flying in is the quickest way to slot Cuenca into a trip that otherwise centres on Quito or the Galápagos. Schedules change, so check current routes with your airline or operator.

By road, Cuenca is reached from the central Andes, for example from Riobamba, the gateway to Chimborazo, which the Instituto Geofísico gives as [6,263 m, the highest volcano in the northern Andes](https://www.igepn.edu.ec/chimborazo). The drive south through the mountains is long but scenic, and breaking it at Ingapirca makes sense. If you are working down from Quito, read our guide to [the Avenue of the Volcanoes and Cotopaxi](/posts/avenue-of-the-volcanoes-and-cotopaxi/) for the stretch before.

### Roads towards the coast

Many travellers think about continuing from Cuenca down to the coast. At the time of writing, the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office [advises against all but essential travel](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance) to the coastal provinces of Esmeraldas, Manabí, Santa Elena, Guayas, El Oro, Los Ríos and Santo Domingo de los Tsáchilas, except airside transit at Guayaquil airport. That covers Guayaquil and the routes down to it, and travel insurance can be invalidated if you travel against FCDO advice. Check the current advice, and your own government's if you are not British, before planning any road travel west or south-west from Cuenca.

The same page reports a 60-day state of emergency for security reasons from 15 September, covering several provinces and some individual cantons. In Azuay, Cuenca's province, the canton listed is Camilo Ponce Enríquez, not Cuenca itself. Check whether the state of emergency has been extended or changed before you travel. The FCDO also notes that the [rainy season usually runs from October to May](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security) and that Ecuadorian authorities have issued a red alert for a forecast 2026–2027 El Niño, so ask your operator about road conditions before long drives.

## How many days to give it

We would give Cuenca two nights at the very least, and three is better. That gives you:

- **Day one**: the old centre, Parque Calderón, the cathedrals and an evening walk along the Tomebamba.
- **Day two**: a morning in Cajas National Park, then a market lunch and an afternoon looking at hats and museums back in town.
- **Day three**: Ingapirca as a day trip, or as a stop on the road north.

If you are flying in and out, two nights covers the city and Cajas. If you are travelling by road from the central Andes, building in Ingapirca on the way down and spending three nights in Cuenca makes the long journey worth it.

## How Cuenca fits a wider trip

Cuenca works well as the southern end of an Andes journey that starts in Quito. A classic shape is a few days in the capital (our [Quito in three days](/posts/quito-in-three-days/) plan covers that), a run south along the volcano road, and then Cuenca, before flying back north. It also makes a calm counterpoint to a Galápagos cruise or an Amazon lodge stay: after days of early starts and wildlife, an unhurried colonial city is a good place to rest.
