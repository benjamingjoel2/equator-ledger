Picture two mornings in the Galápagos. In the first, you wake in a small cabin to the sound of the anchor chain, step out on deck and find an island you have never seen before, close enough to hear the sea lions. In the second, you wake in a guesthouse in Puerto Ayora, buy a coffee on the waterfront, watch the fishing boats come in and wander down to the harbour to meet your day boat. Both are real Galápagos mornings. They lead to quite different trips.

The question we hear most from people planning the islands is which one to choose. There is no universally right answer, but there are honest trade-offs, and once you see them laid out most people know quickly which side they fall on. Here is how we think about it.

## The two ways to see the islands

A **cruise** means living aboard a boat that moves between islands, usually overnight, so that each day starts somewhere new. Ecuador's tourism ministry describes [regulated cruises with certified naturalist guides, with overnight trips running from 3 to 14 nights](https://ecuador.travel/galapagos/). You eat, sleep and travel on the boat, and go ashore by small dinghy (a panga) for guided walks and snorkelling.

A **land-based trip**, often called island-hopping, means sleeping in hotels or guesthouses on the inhabited islands and moving between them by public or private boat. From each base you take guided day trips to nearby visitor sites, snorkel from beaches, and spend evenings in town. The bases are the inhabited islands: Santa Cruz, San Cristóbal, Isabela and Floreana.

Both start the same way. You fly from Quito or Guayaquil to one of the two island airports, on Baltra (for Santa Cruz) or on San Cristóbal, according to [Ecuador Travel](https://ecuador.travel/galapagos/). From there, the paths split.

## Reach: where each style can take you

This is the biggest difference, and the one that should weigh most if wildlife is your main reason for going.

The more remote islands, such as Genovesa, Española and Fernandina, are typically cruise territory. They lie far enough from the towns that reaching them and coming back in a day is usually impractical, and visits there are generally made from boats that sail overnight. If you have your heart set on the seabird colonies of Genovesa, the albatrosses of Española or the lava fields and marine iguanas of Fernandina, ask any operator directly whether a land-based plan can reach them. The answer is usually no.

<Photo name="genovesa-nazca-booby" caption="A Nazca booby on Genovesa, one of the islands usually reached by cruise." />

A land-based trip, on the other hand, gives you real depth on the inhabited islands. On Santa Cruz you can walk out to Tortuga Bay, visit the Charles Darwin Research Station and look for giant tortoises in the highlands near Los Gemelos. On San Cristóbal, Kicker Rock (León Dormido) is a classic snorkelling trip. On Isabela, Las Tintoreras and the Sierra Negra volcano are within reach. Day boats from the towns also visit some nearby uninhabited islands, though which ones, and how often, varies, so ask.

In short: a cruise trades depth for breadth. A land trip trades breadth for depth.

## Seasickness and the sea itself

Be honest with yourself here. On a cruise, much of the travelling between islands happens at night, and open-ocean crossings can be lively. Some people sleep through it; others spend the first night wishing they had chosen the hotel. Smaller boats tend to move more than larger ones, though no boat is immune.

Land-based trips involve the sea too. Inter-island transfers by speedboat can be fast and bumpy, and day trips to snorkelling sites mean time on small boats. The difference is that you sleep on solid ground, and you can usually choose to skip a rough day.

If you know you suffer badly, talk to your doctor before you go, ask an operator how long the crossings are on the route they propose, and consider a land-based trip or a combination.

## Pace and how a day feels

A cruise day has a rhythm that people either love or find relentless. Typically there is an early landing, a walk with your guide, a return to the boat for a meal, a snorkel or a panga ride, another landing in the afternoon and a briefing on the next day. You see a great deal, and you do it on the boat's schedule. The park's [visiting hours run from 06:00 to 18:00](https://galapagos.gob.ec/download/11826), so days are shaped around the light.

Land-based trips have more give in them. Some days are full excursions; others can be a slow morning, a walk to a beach and a long lunch. You will spend more time in transit between bases, and some of your best hours may be in town rather than at visitor sites. For families with young children, or anyone who wants to set their own pace, that flexibility is worth a lot.

## Evenings, comfort and atmosphere

On a cruise, evenings are spent on board with the same small group of fellow passengers, usually with a briefing from the guide and dinner together. You might be anchored off an island with no lights in sight and a sky full of stars. It is sociable, contained and quiet, and if you do not take to your fellow travellers, there is no escape.

On land, evenings belong to you. Puerto Ayora has a working harbour, a fish market where sea lions and pelicans wait for scraps, and plenty of places to eat. Puerto Baquerizo Moreno on San Cristóbal and Puerto Villamil on Isabela are smaller and quieter. You get a sense of how people actually live in the islands, which a cruise rarely gives you.

## Budget, without the numbers

We do not publish prices because they move with the season, the standard of boat or hotel, the length of trip and how far ahead you book. What we can say is this:

- **Cruises bundle costs.** Accommodation, meals, guiding and transport between islands are usually included, so the headline figure looks large but covers most of what you need.
- **Land trips spread costs out.** Hotels, meals, day tours and inter-island boats are paid separately, so the total can be lower or higher depending on your choices.
- **Some costs apply to everyone.** The Galápagos National Park entry fee and the Transit Control Card are charged however you travel. We explain both in [our guide to Galápagos entry fees and rules](/posts/galapagos-entry-fees-and-rules/).

Whichever you lean towards, ask for a written, itemised quote that shows exactly what is and is not included: flights, park fee, transit card, tips, wetsuit hire and transfers.

## Side by side

| | Cruise | Land-based |
|---|---|---|
| Remote islands | Typically yes | Typically no |
| Nights | On board | In town |
| Pace | Set by the boat | Flexible |
| Seasickness risk | Higher (night crossings) | Lower (day transfers) |
| Evenings | Small group, quiet | Restaurants, harbour life |
| Costs | Mostly bundled | Paid item by item |

## Who each style suits

A cruise tends to suit you if you are there mainly for wildlife, want to reach the outer islands, have limited days and want them used efficiently, and are reasonably confident on the water.

A land-based trip tends to suit you if you are travelling with young children, are prone to seasickness, want to snorkel and swim at your own pace, like to choose where you eat, or are combining the islands with a longer trip and want more flexibility.

If you are timing the trip around particular wildlife or sea conditions, read [our guide to the best time to visit the Galápagos](/posts/best-time-to-visit-the-galapagos/) alongside this one.

## The combined option

You do not have to choose one. A common approach is a short cruise to reach the outer islands, followed by a few nights on Santa Cruz or Isabela to slow down. Or the reverse: a land-based start to find your feet, then a cruise. Because the cruises the tourism ministry describes start at 3 nights, a short boat trip is a realistic part of a longer stay. Ask your operator how the two pieces connect, including where the cruise starts and ends and how you get to your hotel afterwards.

## The rules apply either way

Whether you sail or stay on land, the same park rules shape your days. Under the park's [2024 visitor rules](https://galapagos.gob.ec/reglas-de-visita/), you visit protected sites only with a guide authorised by the Galápagos National Park Directorate, you travel with authorised operators and boats, you keep at least 2 metres from wildlife, you stay on marked trails, and visiting hours are 06:00 to 18:00. Full details are in [Resolución 0000007](https://galapagos.gob.ec/download/11826).

The park updated its visitor rules in 2026. According to press reports from [Lexis](https://www.lexis.com.ec/noticias/registro-oficial-del-dia-nuevas-reglas-de-visita-para-areas-protegidas-de-galapagos), [Vistazo](https://www.vistazo.com/sostenibilidad/2026-09-21-parque-nacional-galapagos-actualiza-reglas-visita-estas-son-nuevas-normas-restricciones-OB11238475) and [Primicias](https://www.primicias.ec/sociedad/parque-nacional-galapagos-visitas-nuevas-reglas-turistas-nacionales-extranjeros-133113/), the new resolution was published in the Registro Oficial on 14 September 2026 and sets new minimum distances, including for kayaks and pangas, as well as advance reservations at some busy sites. Those reported sites include Las Grietas and Volcán Chico, which are popular with land-based visitors, so this matters if you are planning to explore independently. Ask your operator for the current rules in writing.

Two more points apply to everyone. The UK government says [travel health insurance is mandatory for foreign tourists in Galápagos](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/entry-requirements). And at the time of writing, it reports [heavy rain and flooding on Santa Cruz Island](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance), with an emergency declared by Gobierno Galápagos on 31 October 2026, road closures, power cuts and disruption to travel to Baltra airport. Check the latest before you travel, especially if Santa Cruz is your land base or your cruise starts from Baltra.

## Before you book

Whichever way you go, these are the questions we would put to an operator:

- Which islands and visitor sites does this itinerary actually include, and are any of them subject to advance reservations under the 2026 rules?
- On a cruise, how long are the night crossings, and how large is the boat?
- On a land trip, which day excursions are included, and which would I book locally?
- Is everything itemised in writing, including the park fee, Transit Control Card, inter-island transfers and tips?
- What happens if weather or the current Santa Cruz disruption affects a transfer or a flight?
- Can you combine a short cruise with nights on land?
