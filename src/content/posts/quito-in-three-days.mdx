The first thing you notice in Quito is your own breathing. You step out of a taxi into thin, bright mountain air, climb a short cobbled hill towards a church, and halfway up you find yourself stopping to admire a balcony you would normally walk straight past. The city sits in a long Andean valley with green volcanic slopes rising behind it, and the light up here is sharp enough to make the whitewashed walls of the old town glow.

Ecuador's tourism ministry calls Quito [the oldest and highest capital in Latin America](https://ecuador.travel/andes/), and both halves of that description shape a good visit. The history rewards slow walking, and the altitude insists on it. Three days is enough to see the old centre properly, ride up the mountain, eat well and take one day trip out of the city. Here is how we would spend them.

## Before day one: give the altitude its due

Quito is not a place to arrive and sprint. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) notes that [altitude sickness is a risk in parts of Ecuador](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/health), and that UV radiation is a risk too. In practice that means a gentle first afternoon, plenty of water, an early night and no heavy drinking until you know how your body is coping. Headaches, breathlessness and poor sleep are common in the first day or two. If symptoms get worse rather than better, see a doctor and go lower. The FCDO health page also lists 911 as the emergency number.

Pack sun cream, a hat and layers: Quito days swing from warm sunshine to chilly, cloudy afternoons.

On health more broadly, the [CDC's Ecuador page](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/ecuador) says there is no malaria transmission in the city of Quito, and that yellow fever vaccination is not recommended for travel limited to Quito. If your wider trip heads into the Amazon, that changes, so read our [first-timer's guide to Ecuador](/posts/first-timers-guide-to-ecuador/) and talk to a travel health clinic.

## Day one: the colonial old town

Quito's historic centre was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1978, according to [Wikipedia's list of World Heritage Sites in Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Ecuador). Walk into it and you understand why. Narrow streets climb and drop between churches, convents and pastel houses with carved wooden balconies, and every few blocks the street opens into a plaza with mountains framed at the far end.

Start at **Plaza Grande**, the main square. Sit on a bench in the sun and watch the shoe-shiners, office workers and families coming and going around the government palace and the cathedral. From here, the great churches of the old town are all within walking distance. Some are austere from outside and astonishing within, all gilded altarpieces and painted ceilings, and the contrast between bright street and dim, glittering interior is part of the pleasure. Opening hours change, so check on the day.

Take the afternoon slowly. Pick two or three churches rather than trying to tick off every one, stop for coffee, and let the hills dictate your pace. We like ending the day on **La Ronda**, a narrow, restored colonial street of small shops, cafés and balconies hung with flowers. It is at its most atmospheric in the late afternoon and early evening, when the lamps come on and the street fills with people eating and strolling.

On a clear day, the hilltop of **El Panecillo**, crowned by its winged Virgin statue, gives a sweeping view back over the old town. Go by taxi rather than on foot.

### Staying safe in the old town

The old town is busy and full of life, and it is also where you need to be most alert. The FCDO says mugging and pickpocketing are common and specifically advises visitors to [take care in Quito's old town and at transport hubs](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security). Keep your phone out of sight in crowds, carry only what you need, and use a bag that closes and sits in front of you.

The same FCDO page warns that express kidnappings are common, often involving taxis hailed in the street, and that scopolamine drugging has been reported. Its advice is to use registered or radio-dispatched taxis. We would add: ask your hotel to call one, or use a ride you have booked in advance, especially after dark.

## Day two: up the mountain, then into the newer city

### The TelefériQo

On your second morning, once you have a night's acclimatisation behind you, ride the **TelefériQo**, the cable car that climbs the slopes of Pichincha above the city. The FCDO gives the arrival station at Cruz Loma as [4,050 m above sea level](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security), so you will feel it. Walk slowly at the top, drink water, and turn back if you feel unwell.

The reward, if the cloud stays away, is the whole of Quito laid out below you: a long ribbon of city filling the valley floor, with volcanoes beyond. Go early, since mornings are usually clearer and afternoon cloud often rolls in. The Pichincha massif includes Guagua Pichincha, which the Instituto Geofísico describes as an [active volcano 12 km west of Quito](https://www.igepn.edu.ec/guagua-pichincha).

### Markets, parks and the newer city

Come down for lunch and spend the afternoon in the newer, northern part of the city, which has a completely different feel: wide avenues, big parks, galleries and cafés.

Then go to a market. Ecuadorian markets are where the country's astonishing range of produce becomes obvious: piles of potatoes in colours you did not know potatoes came in, tropical fruit from the lowlands sitting beside Andean grains, bunches of herbs, and cooked-food stalls where locals eat at shared counters. Markets are also busy, so keep the same eye on your belongings as you would in the old town.

### What to eat in Quito

Andean food is warming, generous and built around potatoes, corn, cheese and pork. A few dishes to look out for:

- **Locro de papa**: a thick, comforting potato soup, usually with cheese and often served with avocado. On a cool Quito evening it is exactly what you want.
- **Empanadas de viento**: large, puffed-up fried pastries filled with cheese and dusted with sugar, a sweet-and-salty combination that works far better than it sounds.
- **Hornado**: slow-roasted pork, typically served with potato cakes and other accompaniments, and a fixture of markets and food stalls.

Eat where it is busy and the food is cooked fresh in front of you. The FCDO advises drinking boiled, filtered or bottled water, so ask for drinks without ice if you are unsure.

## Day three: a day trip out of the city

Quito makes an excellent base for day trips, and three very different ones are within reach. Pick one: trying to combine them makes for a long day in a vehicle.

### Mitad del Mundo: standing on the equator

North of the city, the **Mitad del Mundo** monument marks the equator, the line that gives Ecuador its name. It is a fun, slightly theatrical half-day: a large monument, a line to stand astride with one foot in each hemisphere, and the obligatory photo. It pairs easily with a relaxed afternoon back in town. We have not linked a website for the monument because we could not find an official one we trust, so ask your hotel or operator about current opening arrangements.

### Otavalo market

North of Quito in Imbabura, **Otavalo** is known for its market, one of the best known in the Andes. Textiles, woven bags, ponchos, blankets, jewellery and crafts fill the stalls and streets, and the surrounding countryside of lakes and volcanoes makes the drive part of the experience. If you want gifts to take home, this is the place. Ask your operator which day the market is at its fullest, and consider going with a driver or a guide so you can stop at viewpoints on the way.

### Mindo cloud forest

West of Quito, the road drops away from the high Andes into the **Mindo** cloud forest, a world of moss, ferns, orchids, rivers and birds. Cloud forest is mountain forest that spends much of its time wrapped in mist, and it feels softer, greener and more humid than Quito. Hummingbirds are the stars: at feeders you can watch several species darting past within arm's length. Birdwatching, short forest walks and waterfalls fill the day. It is also a gentle change of altitude, which some travellers welcome after a couple of days up high.

## The current security picture

The FCDO reports a [60-day state of emergency for security reasons from 15 September](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance) in eight provinces, including Pichincha, the province that contains Quito. A state of emergency can bring extra police and military on the streets and, in some areas, restrictions such as curfews. Sixty days from mid-September runs to around the middle of November, so check whether it has been extended or changed before you travel, and keep an eye on local news while you are there.

The same page advises against all but essential travel to several coastal provinces, so if you are thinking of adding a beach to a Quito trip, read the current advice first (and your own government's, if you are not British). Travel insurance can be invalidated if you travel against FCDO advice.

## How Quito fits a wider trip

Most visitors pass through Quito at least once, on the way to the Galápagos, the volcano road or the Amazon. Three days lets you adjust to the altitude while seeing the city properly. From here, the obvious next step south is the [Avenue of the Volcanoes and Cotopaxi](/posts/avenue-of-the-volcanoes-and-cotopaxi/), and further south still, [Cuenca and the southern Andes](/posts/cuenca-and-the-southern-andes/) offer a quieter colonial city to compare with the capital.

If the Galápagos are part of your plan, a couple of nights in Quito before the flight makes a good buffer against delays. Read [Galápagos entry fees and rules, explained](/posts/galapagos-entry-fees-and-rules/) before you go, as the paperwork starts at the mainland airport.
