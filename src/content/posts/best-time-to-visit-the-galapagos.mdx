The Galápagos sit on the equator, so you might expect the same weather all year. They do not have it. Stand on a beach on Santa Cruz in March and the air is still and warm, the sky is a hard blue, and the water feels like an invitation. Stand in the same spot in August and a soft grey lid of cloud may hang over the island, the breeze has an edge to it, and the sea, cooler now, seems busier with life.

Neither of those is the wrong time to go. The islands have two seasons, each with real pleasures and real drawbacks, and the best time for you depends on what you most want to do there: swim in calm, warm water, watch seabirds and sea lions at their busiest, or simply find a cabin on the boat you want. Here is how we would think it through.

## The two seasons, in brief

Ecuador's tourism ministry divides the Galápagos year into two. The [warm season runs from December to May](https://ecuador.travel/como-llegar-a-galapagos/), with sunny days, occasional rain and warm, clear water. The cooler season runs from June to November. The ministry calls it the dry season, and says it is cooler because of the Humboldt Current and brings more marine activity.

The turn between the two is not abrupt. May and June, and November and December, often have something of each, and every year is a little different. Treat the seasons as a broad guide, not a timetable.

## The warm season: December to May

On land, this is the season of bright, hot days. The light is strong, the shade is welcome, and the islands, especially the higher ground on the larger islands, can look noticeably greener after showers. The ministry describes the rain as occasional; when it does come, it can still disrupt a landing or a road.

On the water, this is the season people dream about. The sea is generally calmer and the water clearer and warmer, which makes snorkelling more comfortable and suits people who are newer to it. Many travellers find they can spend longer in the water without getting cold. Crossings between islands also tend to be gentler, which matters if you are prone to seasickness and weighing a cruise; we cover that trade-off in [our guide to choosing a cruise or island-hopping](/posts/galapagos-cruise-or-island-hopping/).

The downsides: the midday sun is fierce, so hikes on exposed lava are best done early, and the official [park visiting hours of 06:00 to 18:00](https://galapagos.gob.ec/download/11826) help you plan around the heat. The UK government also notes that [UV radiation is a risk in Ecuador](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/health), so pack proper sun protection.

## The cool season: June to November

On land, the cool season feels milder and greyer. Cloud often sits over the islands, especially in the mornings and on the higher ground, and a fine mist can drift through the highlands. For walking, many people find it more comfortable than the warm months: less glare, less heat, and a landscape that turns browner and starker on the lowlands.

On the water, it is a different experience. The cooler water carried by the Humboldt Current, which the ministry associates with more marine activity, makes the sea feel full: seabirds diving, sea lions on every rock, fish in shoals. The price is comfort. The water is colder, so most people want a wetsuit for snorkelling, and the sea is often rougher, with more swell on open crossings. If you or anyone in your group struggles with motion, think carefully about a long cruise in these months.

## What about the wildlife?

Here we want to be careful, because the internet is full of month-by-month wildlife calendars, and nature does not always read them. A few general points we are comfortable with:

- **Much of the famous wildlife is there all year.** Sea lions, marine iguanas, giant tortoises, blue-footed boobies and Galápagos penguins can be seen in both seasons. What changes is behaviour: courtship, nesting and the arrival of young happen at different times for different species, and they shift from year to year.
- **Some species are seasonal visitors.** The waved albatrosses of Española, the bird on this page, are the clearest example: they come to the island to breed and spend part of the year out at sea. If seeing them matters to you, ask your operator about timing for your dates, and check that your route actually visits Española, which is typically reached by cruise.
- **The sea follows the seasons.** The ministry links the cool season with more marine activity, and many snorkellers notice the difference in the water. Warm-season snorkelling, though, has its own reward in visibility and comfort.

Whatever you see, remember the park's rules: under the [2024 visitor rules](https://galapagos.gob.ec/download/11826), you keep at least 2 metres from animals, do not touch or feed them, and do not use flash. The park updated its rules in 2026, so ask your operator for the current version in writing; we explain the change in [our guide to Galápagos entry fees and rules](/posts/galapagos-entry-fees-and-rules/).

## Crowds, holidays and availability

Season is only half the question. The other half is demand. Boats and hotels in the Galápagos are limited, and they fill fastest around the times when families can travel: the Christmas and New Year period, Easter, and the northern-hemisphere summer holidays. Ecuadorian and other South American school and public holidays add to demand at other points in the year.

What this means in practice:

- **If your dates are fixed by school holidays**, book early, especially for a particular cruise or a small boat with a specific route.
- **If you are flexible**, ask your operator which weeks look quieter for your preferred style of trip. Quieter does not always mean less to see.
- **Prices move with demand**, and we do not publish them. Ask for a written, itemised quote for your exact dates, and compare the same trip across two or three possible weeks if you can.

## El Niño and this year's weather

Two current notes matter for anyone travelling in the coming months.

First, the UK government reports that the Ecuadorian authorities have issued a [red alert for the forecast 2026–2027 El Niño](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security). It also notes that the rainy season in Ecuador usually runs from October to May. El Niño years can bring heavier rain and warmer seas than usual, which can mean disrupted roads, flights and landings, and changes in where wildlife feeds. We would not cancel a trip on the strength of a forecast, but we would build in some slack, choose flexible bookings where possible, and make sure your travel insurance covers weather disruption.

Second, at the time of writing, the UK government reports [heavy rain and flooding on Santa Cruz Island](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance), with an emergency declared by Gobierno Galápagos on 31 October 2026, road closures, power cuts and disruption to travel to Baltra airport. Many trips pass through Santa Cruz, and Baltra is one of the two arrival airports, so this can affect both cruises and land-based trips. Check the latest advice before you travel and ask your operator how they are handling transfers.

## So when should you go?

Our honest summary:

- **Go in the warm season (December to May)** if you want calm, warm water for snorkelling, gentler crossings, and blue skies, and you can handle strong sun.
- **Go in the cool season (June to November)** if you are drawn to busier seas and seabird activity, prefer milder temperatures for walking, and do not mind a wetsuit and some swell.
- **Go when you can get the trip you want.** For most people the deciding factor is not weather but availability, budget and how the Galápagos fit into a wider trip.

## Before you book

Questions worth putting to any operator about your dates:

- What are sea conditions usually like on this route at this time of year, and how long are the open crossings?
- Will I want a wetsuit, and is one included or available to hire?
- If I want to see a particular species, such as the waved albatross, is my timing realistic, and does the route visit the right island?
- How busy are these weeks, and would moving a week either way make a difference to availability?
- How are you handling the current Santa Cruz flooding and any El Niño disruption, and what happens if a flight or landing is cancelled?
- Can you send a written, itemised quote, including the park entry fee and Transit Control Card?
