The first thing you notice is the sound. Step off the canoe, climb the bank, and the air is thick and warm and loud: insects in layers, frogs somewhere close, a bird call that sounds like a dripping tap, and under it all the slow movement of the river. After the thin, cold air of the Andes, the rainforest feels like walking into a greenhouse that is alive.

A stay in an Amazon lodge is one of Ecuador's great experiences, and it is gentler than many people expect. You don't need to be an explorer. You need curiosity, decent rain gear and a willingness to get up early. This guide covers what a typical stay is like, two of the main lodge areas, how you get there, the health advice that matters, and the questions we'd ask before booking.

## What a lodge day is like

Every lodge runs its own programme, but the rhythm is broadly similar, and it is set by the forest rather than the clock.

**Before dawn.** A knock on the door or a quiet alarm while it is still dark. Coffee, then out on foot or by canoe as the light comes up, because the first hours of the day are when birds and monkeys are most active. Mornings on the water are often the highlight: mist lifting off the river, parrots crossing overhead in noisy pairs, and the guide stopping the paddle to point at something you would never have seen.

**Mid-morning.** A walk on forest trails, slow and close to the ground. This is when a good guide earns their keep, explaining which plants are used as medicine or building material, finding insects disguised as leaves, and reading tracks in the mud.

**Midday.** Lunch and a rest through the heat. Hammocks were invented for exactly this hour.

**Late afternoon.** Back on the water or up a canopy tower, if the lodge has one, as the forest wakes again. Sunsets over a lagoon or a wide river bend are worth staying out for.

**Night.** After dinner, many lodges offer a night walk or a canoe trip with torches, looking for frogs, spiders, sleeping birds and the eyeshine of caimans. Then bed, under a mosquito net, with the forest louder than ever. The night chorus can be startling the first evening and oddly soothing by the third.

**Rain.** It will rain. Sometimes a short, heavy downpour that drums on the roof and stops as suddenly as it started; sometimes a grey, soaking afternoon. Activities usually carry on in the wet. The rain is part of the place, and some of the most atmospheric hours you spend there will be under a dripping roof, listening.

We'd recommend at least three nights. With travel days at each end, anything shorter leaves you with only one or two full days in the forest.

## Yasuní

[Yasuní was designated a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve in 1989](https://www.unesco.org/en/mab/yasuni), and UNESCO describes it as one of the most biodiverse areas per square metre on the planet. Ecuador's tourism ministry says [Yasuní National Park](https://ecuador.travel/amazonia/) lies between the provinces of Orellana and Pastaza, is the largest protected area in mainland Ecuador, is home to more than 1,400 animal species, and includes Waorani territory.

What that means on the ground is big rivers, tall terra firme forest, clay licks where parrots gather, and a real chance of monkeys, giant otters, and birds you will be looking up for years afterwards. Some lodges in and around Yasuní are run with or by Indigenous communities, which can add a great deal to a stay (see the questions further down).

## Cuyabeno

Cuyabeno, in the north-east, is a different kind of place. The tourism ministry describes it as a [reserve of lagoons](https://ecuador.travel/amazonia/) where dolphins, manatees and caimans can be seen. Much of the forest here floods, so you spend a lot of time in canoes, gliding between drowned trees and out onto open water that turns gold and pink at sunset.

There is an important safety point. The usual road and air gateway towards Cuyabeno is the town of Lago Agrio (Nueva Loja), which the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) says lies [inside the 20 km zone along the Colombian border](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/regional-risks). The FCDO [advises against all but essential travel](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance) to areas within 20 km of that border, and travelling against FCDO advice can affect your insurance. For Cuyabeno itself, the FCDO says to take local advice, travel in daylight with a reputable official guide, and have an emergency plan.

If you are considering Cuyabeno, read the current advice from your own government, ask your operator exactly how you would reach the lodge and by which route, and check with your insurer before you book.

## Getting there

Most Amazon lodge stays begin in Quito. From there you either take a domestic flight to a small Amazon town or travel by road down the eastern slope of the Andes, and then continue by river. Many lodges are reached only by canoe or motorised boat, so the last part of the journey is often the most memorable. Encantadas Expeditions, a local operator, describes its lodges on its [homepage](https://encantadasexpeditions.tourground.com/) as "reached by canoe, a 35-minute flight from the capital".

Journey times, connections and luggage limits on small boats vary from lodge to lodge, so ask for the full door-to-door plan in writing. The road route can be combined with Baños and the [Avenue of the Volcanoes](/posts/avenue-of-the-volcanoes-and-cotopaxi/), dropping from high mountains to rainforest in a single day.

## Health: vaccines, malaria and mosquitoes

The rainforest is where Ecuador's health advice matters most. Check with a travel health clinic at least several weeks before you travel. The key points from the [US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/ecuador) are:

- **Yellow fever.** The CDC recommends vaccination for travellers aged nine months or older going to areas below 2,300 m east of the Andes in the provinces of Morona-Santiago, Napo, Orellana, Pastaza, Sucumbíos, Tungurahua and Zamora-Chinchipe. That covers the main Amazon lodge areas. Ecuador's tourism ministry also [recommends the vaccine](https://ecuador.travel/como-llegar-a-galapagos/) at least 10 days before visiting the Amazon provinces of Orellana, Sucumbíos, Pastaza, Napo, Morona Santiago and Zamora Chinchipe.
- **Malaria.** The CDC says transmission occurs below 1,500 m in Cotopaxi, Esmeraldas, Morona-Santiago, Orellana, Pastaza and Sucumbíos, with rare cases elsewhere below 1,500 m. It recommends preventive medication for those six provinces, and mosquito-bite avoidance elsewhere. Ask your clinic which applies to the lodge you choose.
- **Routine vaccines.** Make sure these are up to date. The CDC also recommends hepatitis A for unvaccinated travellers aged one or older and hepatitis B for unvaccinated travellers under 60, and suggests considering typhoid and rabies depending on your trip.

Whatever your medication, preventing bites is your first line of defence. Wear long sleeves and trousers at dawn and dusk, use an effective repellent, and sleep under the net provided. The CDC currently lists a global dengue notice, and the [FCDO health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/health) mentions Oropouche virus, both spread by insect bites. The FCDO also advises drinking boiled, filtered or bottled water.

Lodges are remote and medical care is far away, so make sure your travel insurance covers emergency evacuation, and tell the lodge about any medical conditions before you arrive.

## What to pack

Pack light: small boats and planes often have tight luggage limits, and you will live in the same few clothes.

- Lightweight long-sleeved shirts and long trousers, quick-drying and in muted colours
- A good waterproof jacket, and a dry bag for camera and phone
- Rubber boots for muddy trails (ask whether the lodge lends them, and in what sizes)
- Sandals or light shoes for around the lodge
- A head torch with spare batteries
- Binoculars: the single item that most improves a rainforest stay
- Insect repellent, sunscreen, a hat and any medication you need
- A warm layer for the journey through the Andes, and for early mornings on the river
- Cash in small notes for tips and community craft purchases (the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security) notes that US dollars are Ecuador's currency)

## Questions to ask a lodge

Amazon lodges vary widely, from simple community-run cabins to more comfortable eco-lodges, and the differences are not always obvious from the photographs. We don't recommend specific lodges here; instead, these are the questions we'd put to any lodge or operator:

- **Ownership.** Who owns the lodge? Is it owned or co-run by an Indigenous community, and how do they share in its income and decisions?
- **Guiding.** Will we have a local or Indigenous guide as well as a bilingual naturalist? How many guests share each guide?
- **Location.** Which reserve or park is the lodge in, which province, and what is the exact route there? Does it pass through any area covered by FCDO warnings?
- **Programme.** What does a typical day include? Are canoe trips, night walks and any canopy tower included, or extra?
- **Safety.** What is the emergency plan, how far is the nearest clinic, and how does the lodge communicate with the outside world?
- **Price.** What is included (transfers, meals, drinks, park entry, guiding), and can we have it as a written, itemised quote? Prices vary a lot, so compare like with like.
