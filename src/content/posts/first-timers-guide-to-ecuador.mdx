The first thing you notice in Quito is your own breathing. You climb a short cobbled street towards Plaza Grande and your legs are fine, but your lungs want a pause at the top. That small, slightly comic moment is a good introduction to the whole country: Ecuador is compact on the map and enormous in how much it changes from one day's travel to the next.

Within a fortnight you can go from a colonial capital in the Andes to a lake in a volcanic crater, down through cloud forest and rainforest, and out to islands where sea lions sleep on the benches. This guide is the one we wish every first-timer read before booking: how the country fits together, how long to give it, and the rules on entry, money, health and safety, each linked to the official source.

## How the country fits together

Ecuador's tourism ministry sums the country up as ["one destination, four worlds"](https://ecuador.travel/): the Andes, the coast, the Amazon and the Galápagos. We go through each in more depth in [Ecuador's four worlds](/posts/ecuadors-four-worlds/), but in short:

- **The Andes** run down the middle of the country. This is where you'll find Quito, Otavalo market, Cotopaxi, the Quilotoa crater lake, Baños and, in the south, Cuenca. Most first trips start here.
- **The Amazon** lies east of the mountains. You stay in a lodge, travel by canoe and spend your days on forest trails and rivers.
- **The coast** lies west of the mountains and includes Guayaquil. For now, UK government advice makes this the region most visitors pass through rather than visit (see the safety section below).
- **The Galápagos** sit about 1,000 km offshore, reached only by air from Quito or Guayaquil, according to [Ecuador Travel](https://ecuador.travel/galapagos/).

Between the Andes and the lowlands sits the cloud forest, with Mindo the best-known base: mossy, misty and full of hummingbirds. It makes an easy first step down from Quito.

## How long to go for

Our honest answer: longer than you think. Ecuador's distances are short, but mountain roads are slow, and the islands need dedicated days at each end for flights and formalities.

- **One week** works for the Andes alone (Quito, a night or two near Cotopaxi or Quilotoa, perhaps Otavalo), or for the Galápagos alone with a night in Quito either side.
- **Ten days** lets you combine Quito and the highlands with either the Amazon or a short Galápagos stay, but not comfortably both.
- **Two weeks** is the length we'd recommend for a first visit that includes the islands and the mainland. Our [14-day Ecuador and Galápagos itinerary](/posts/14-day-ecuador-and-galapagos-itinerary/) shows how it fits.

If Cuenca appeals, give it two nights at least and see [Cuenca and the southern Andes](/posts/cuenca-and-the-southern-andes/). It is a city that rewards slowing down.

## Entry rules

These points come from the UK government's [Ecuador entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/entry-requirements) and apply to British passport holders. If you hold another passport, check your own government's advice, as the rules can differ.

- Your passport must be valid for at least 6 months after the date you arrive.
- You can visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 12-month period.
- At the border you may be asked why you are visiting and to show proof of a return or onward journey, so keep your onward ticket to hand.
- For the Galápagos, FCDO lists extra requirements: evidence of a hotel booking or invitation letter, a return flight to the mainland, the transit control card bought before check-in, and travel health insurance, which FCDO says is mandatory for foreign tourists.

Rules change, so check the page again shortly before you fly.

## Money

Ecuador uses the US dollar. FCDO's [safety and security page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security) says US dollars and centavo coins minted in Ecuador are the only legal currencies. If you can, arrive with some dollars already in hand, and keep a few smaller notes for markets and taxis.

We don't give prices for hotels, lodges, cruises or guides, because they vary widely by season, standard and how far ahead you book. Ask your operator for a written, itemised quote, and check what is and isn't included, especially in the Galápagos, where park fees are often paid separately.

## Health and altitude

Altitude is the thing most first-timers underestimate. FCDO's [health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/health) notes that altitude sickness is a risk in parts of Ecuador, along with strong UV. Plan an easy first day in Quito, drink plenty of water and leave the big walks and high viewpoints for later in the week. The TelefériQo cable car takes you to an arrival station at 4,050 m, according to [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/safety-and-security), which is a big step up if you've just landed.

For vaccines and medicines, the [CDC's Ecuador page](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/ecuador) is detailed and depends on where you go:

- **Yellow fever:** vaccination is recommended for travellers aged 9 months or older going to areas below 2,300 m east of the Andes, which takes in the Amazon provinces. It is not recommended for travel limited to areas above 2,300 m, the cities of Quito or Guayaquil, or the Galápagos.
- **Malaria:** CDC reports transmission below 1,500 m in Cotopaxi, Esmeraldas, Morona-Santiago, Orellana, Pastaza and Sucumbíos, and recommends preventive medication for those provinces. It reports no transmission in Quito, Guayaquil or the Galápagos.
- **Routine vaccines:** be up to date before you go. CDC also recommends hepatitis A for most travellers.

See a travel health clinic well before departure, ideally with your route in hand. FCDO advises drinking boiled, filtered or bottled water, and the emergency number in Ecuador is 911.

## Safety

Read the [FCDO warnings page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance) before you book, not after. At the time of writing it says:

- FCDO advises against all but essential travel to the coastal provinces of Esmeraldas, Manabí, Santa Elena, Guayas, El Oro, Los Ríos and Santo Domingo de los Tsáchilas. This does not apply to airside transit at Guayaquil airport. Travelling against FCDO advice can invalidate your travel insurance.
- FCDO also advises against all but essential travel within 20 km of the Colombian border, with some exceptions in Carchi. Lago Agrio (Nueva Loja) lies inside that zone. For Cuyabeno in the Amazon, FCDO's [regional risks page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/regional-risks) says to take local advice, travel in daylight with a reputable official guide and have an emergency plan.
- A 60-day state of emergency for security reasons was declared from 15 September in eight provinces, including Pichincha, where Quito is, and Guayas. Check whether it has been extended.

In the cities, the everyday risks are more ordinary. FCDO says mugging and pickpocketing are common and to take care in Quito's old town and at transport hubs. Keep your phone out of sight on busy streets, carry only what you need, and use registered or radio-dispatched taxis rather than hailing one in the street. A hotel or restaurant will call one for you.

Please also check your own government's advice. Other countries may draw different lines.

## Getting around

Most international flights arrive in Quito or Guayaquil. For a first trip we'd base the mainland part in Quito and the highlands, which keeps you clear of the coastal provinces covered by the FCDO warning.

- **Private driver or guide:** the easiest way to see the Avenue of the Volcanoes, Cotopaxi and Quilotoa in a few days. See [the Avenue of the Volcanoes and Cotopaxi](/posts/avenue-of-the-volcanoes-and-cotopaxi/).
- **Domestic flights:** useful for longer hops, such as Quito to Cuenca, and the only way to reach the Galápagos.
- **Buses:** they reach most towns, but they are slow on mountain roads, and FCDO's transport hub warning applies. Daylight journeys are the more comfortable choice.
- **The Amazon:** usually a flight or a long road transfer, then a motorised canoe. Lodges generally arrange this as part of the stay; see [staying in an Amazon lodge](/posts/staying-in-an-amazon-lodge-in-ecuador/).

## The Galápagos in brief

The islands deserve their own planning, and we cover it in [Galápagos cruise or island-hopping?](/posts/galapagos-cruise-or-island-hopping/) and [Galápagos entry fees and rules, explained](/posts/galapagos-entry-fees-and-rules/). The essentials:

- **Transit control card (TCT):** compulsory for every tourist, US$20 per trip, bought at Quito or Guayaquil airport before check-in after online pre-registration, according to the [Galápagos government council (CGREG)](https://www.gobiernogalapagos.gob.ec/preguntas-y-respuestas-frecuentes-control-migratorio-tct/). CGREG also says tourists can stay up to 60 days a year in total.
- **Park entry fee:** US$200 for foreign tourists over 12 and US$100 under 12, collected by park rangers at Baltra and San Cristóbal airports, according to the [Galápagos National Park](https://galapagos.gob.ec/tributo-de-ingreso/). Carry it in cash.
- **Biosecurity declaration:** since 31 January 2026 it is digital only and can be completed up to 72 hours before travel, according to the [Galápagos biosecurity agency (ABG)](https://abgalapagos.gob.ec/galapagos-retira-el-formulario-fisico-de-declaracion-juramentada-de-mercancias/).
- **Via Guayaquil:** FCDO says to check with your airline that you will stay airside.

FCDO currently reports heavy rain and flooding on Santa Cruz Island, with disruption to travel to Baltra airport, so check the [warnings page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ecuador/warnings-and-insurance) close to your dates. For timing, see [the best time to visit the Galápagos](/posts/best-time-to-visit-the-galapagos/).

## Choosing an operator

A good local operator does more than book rooms. It knows which roads are slow in the rainy season, which Amazon lodges are reached by which route, and how the Galápagos rules apply to your particular boat or hotel. We'd look for:

- A company based in Ecuador that plans both the mainland and the islands, so the joins between them are handled by one team.
- Written confirmation of current fees and rules, especially for the Galápagos, where rules and fees can change between booking and travel.
- Clear answers on safety: how routes avoid areas covered by FCDO warnings, and what the plan is if something changes.

## Before you book

Questions worth putting to any operator before you pay a deposit:

1. Can you send a written, itemised quote, showing what is included and what I pay locally, such as the Galápagos entry fee and transit control card?
2. Does any part of the route enter a province covered by an FCDO warning, and if so, why?
3. How is the first day planned to help with the altitude?
4. Which parts of the trip need yellow fever or malaria advice from my clinic?
5. If we connect through Guayaquil for the Galápagos, will we stay airside?
6. What happens if a flight, road or park site is disrupted?

Get the answers in writing, check the official pages again a week before you fly, and then go and catch your breath at the top of that first cobbled street.
